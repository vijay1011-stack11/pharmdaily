What a local anaesthetic is

Definition, history, classification, LA vs GA, and how LAs block a nerve.

Common essay question ★ Exam

Definition

Local anaesthetic (LA)
  • Applied topically or injected locally
  • Causes reversible loss of sensation, especially pain
  • Only in a restricted area, near the site of application
  • Blocks generation and conduction of nerve impulses at any part of the neurone it touches
  • No loss of consciousness · no structural damage
LA on a mixed nerve
↓
Sensory and motor impulses both blocked
↓
Muscle paralysis + loss of autonomic control

History

Coca leaves — chewed by South American natives for stimulant, euphoric effect
↓1860
Albert Niemann isolates cocaine, the first LA; notices it numbs the tongue
↓
Sigmund Freud tries it as a psychic energiser — unsuccessful
↓1884
Carl Koller uses cocaine for ocular surgery — first clinical LA use
↓
Halstead — infiltration anaesthesia
↓1905
Procaine — first synthetic LA
↓1948
Lidocaine — now the most widely used LA

Trick: amides have two i's in the name; esters have one.
  • Amides: lIdocaIne, bupIvacaIne, prIlocaIne, ropIvacaIne, dIbucaIne, mepIvacaIne

Amides (vs esters)

  • More intense, longer anaesthesia
  • Bind α1-acid glycoprotein in plasma
  • Not hydrolysed by plasma esterases → liver
  • Hypersensitivity rare
  • No cross-sensitivity with esters

Esters

  • Short action, weaker analgesia
  • Higher risk of hypersensitivity
  • Rarely used for infiltration or nerve block
  • Still used topically on mucous membranes
For your clinicYour injection (lidocaine) is an amide; your topical gel (benzocaine) is an ester. A patient allergic to one class can usually get the other: no cross-sensitivity between them.

Mechanism of action ★ Exam

The block, step by step

LA reaches the nerve fibre
↓
↓ Na⁺ entry during the upstroke of the action potential
↓as concentration rises
↓ Rate of rise and maximum depolarisation
↓
Conduction slows
↓
Depolarisation fails to reach threshold → conduction block

Route to the receptor

Base form (B)
Penetrates the axon → potency follows lipid solubility of B
Cationic form (BH⁺)
The main active species → binds the receptor
Ratio of B : BH⁺
Set by the drug's pKa

Na⁺ channel states

Resting — activation (m) gate closed
↓threshold depolarisation
Activated — m gate opens, Na⁺ rushes in
↓within a few ms
Inactivated — inactivation (h) gate closes
↓K⁺ efflux repolarises
Back to resting, time-dependent
m gate
Activation gate, extracellular mouth
h gate
Inactivation gate, intracellular mouth
LA prefers
Activated and inactivated states over resting
LA does
Stabilises the inactivated state → slower recovery → ↑ refractory period
Channel structure
  • 300 kD glycoprotein: one large α + two small β1, β2 subunits
  • α subunit: 4 domains (I–IV), each with 6 helical segments (S1–S6)
  • Pore wall: the four S5–S6 segments
  • S4 = voltage sensors → open the activation gate
  • Loop between domains III and IV = inactivation gate
  • LA receptor: S6 of domain IV

Key facts about the block

Critical length
Block needs channels blocked over 2–3 nodes of Ranvier (myelinated fibres)
Frequency-dependent
More block at higher stimulation frequency · resting nerve resists block · repeated stimulation speeds it up
Calcium
↑ Ca⁺⁺ around the fibre → less block
Not hyperpolarisation
Resting membrane potential unchanged · K⁺ channels blocked only at high concentration

pKa decides onset

Low pKa 7.6–7.830–40% base at pH 7.4↓Fast onsetlidocaine, mepivacaine
High pKa 8.1–8.9≤15% base at pH 7.4↓Slow onsetprocaine, tetracaine, bupivacaine
Exception: chloroprocaine — rapid onset despite pKa 9.1

Which fibres go first ★ Exam

Order of block

1 · Pain first lost
2 · Cold
3 · Warmth
4 · Touch
5 · Deep pressure
6 · Motor last lost
  • Recovery: in reverse order — first blocked, last to recover
  • Tongue: bitter → sweet and sour → salty last
  • Autonomic fibres are more sensitive than somatic

Fibre types

Type C and B most susceptible
Small Aδ
Type Aα, Aβ, Aγ least
Myelination
Same diameter: myelinated blocked before non-myelinated
Diameter
Smaller usually more sensitive, but not always:
  • shorter critical length
  • shorter internodes; LA enters at nodes
  • high Na⁺ channel density at nodes
  • small sensory fibres fire at high frequency → frequency-dependent block
Position in the trunk
Outer fibres blocked before core → proximal areas numb first · motor fibres often lie outside, so may block before core sensory fibres
Differential block
Epidural bupivacaine: sensory block at a lower concentration than motor
  • Labour: mother can still push
  • Post-op (knee replacement): early walking and exercises

Why LA fails in inflamed tissue

Inflamed tissue, e.g. infected tooth
↓
a. ↓ Tissue pH → more LA ionised → less diffuses into the axon
↓
b. ↑ Blood flow → LA washed away faster
↓
c. Adrenaline in the LA works less well
↓
d. Inflammatory products oppose LA action
↓
Poor pain control
For your clinicAn abscessed tooth often stays sore after an infiltration: acid pus keeps the LA ionised. That's why a block away from the inflamed site works better than injecting into it.

Prilocaine

  • Like lignocaine, but no vasodilatation at the site
  • Lower CNS toxicity: larger volume of distribution, faster clearance
  • Methaemoglobinaemia: aromatic ring metabolised to o-toluidine
  • Infiltration, nerve block, spinal, IV regional

  • Before IV cannulation, split-skin graft harvesting, superficial procedures

Ropivacaine

  • Newer bupivacaine congener: nearly as long-acting, less cardiotoxic
  • Blocks Aδ and C (pain) more than Aβ (motor)
  • Lower lipid solubility → enters large motor fibres less → lower CNS and cardiac toxicity
  • S(−) enantiomer · needs slightly higher concentrations than bupivacaine
  • Best sensory–motor separation in epidurals → post-op and labour pain
  • Less cardiotoxic → preferred for large-volume nerve blocks · 0.2% injection

Surface-only drugs

Tetracaine

PABA ester · amethocaine

Highly lipid-soluble, more potent and toxic: slow hydrolysis by pseudocholinesterase. Topical use limited to eye, nose, throat.

Not for bronchoscopy: tracheobronchial spray absorbs almost like IV.

Proparacaine

Commonest eye anaesthetic

Meta-aminobenzoic acid derivative. Faster than tetracaine, equally effective. Tonometry 30 s after one 0.5% drop. Cornea numb 10–20 min.

Cataract: 1–2 drops every 5–10 min, max 5. Foreign body or suture removal: 2–3 applications. Minimal irritation, few allergies.

Dibucaine

Most potent, toxic, longest

Cinchocaine. Surface use on less delicate mucosa or skin: ear canal, anal canal (1.1% ear drops). Spinal use abandoned.

Oxethazaine

Works in stomach acid

Ionises very little even at low pH → anaesthetises gastric mucosa. With antacids for gastritis, drug-induced gastric irritation, reflux, heartburn of pregnancy.

0.2% suspension, 5–10 ml orally · >100 mg/day: dizziness, drowsiness

More LAs from college notes

Mepivacaine

Amide · injectable

Similar to lignocaine in onset and duration, but less vasodilatation, so it can be used without a vasoconstrictor (3% plain in dentistry). Nerve blocks.

Articaine

Amide · dental

Amide with a thiophene ring and an ester side chain, so it is partly broken down in plasma and has a short half-life. Dental 4% with adrenaline.

Etidocaine

Amide · long-acting

Long-acting, highly lipid-soluble amide. Produces marked motor block relative to sensory block.

Benoxinate

Ester · surface, eye

Oxybuprocaine. Eye drops (0.4%) for tonometry and minor eye procedures.

Dyclonine

Surface · mucosa

Topical anaesthetic for mouth and throat: lozenges, sprays. Chemically a ketone, not an ester or amide.

2. Infiltration anaesthesia

  • Dilute LA injected into and around the operation site → nerve endings blocked
  • No individual nerves identified
  • Onset almost immediate
  • Shorter than nerve block: lidocaine 30–60 min, bupivacaine 90–180 min
  • Minor operations: incisions, excisions, suturing, hydrocele, herniorrhaphy
  • Dental tooth extraction, root canal treatment, periodontal procedures
  • Drugs: lidocaine, bupivacaine, procaine · with adrenaline
  • Needs a lot of drug for the area covered · motor function spared

3. Conduction block

  • LA around nerve trunks → area distal to the injection is anaesthetised and paralysed
  • Lidocaine 1–2% usual · bupivacaine for longer blocks

5. Epidural anaesthesia

  • Injected into the epidural space: lumbar, sacral (caudal), thoracic or cervical
  • Obstetric and surgical procedures · bupivacaine, lidocaine, ropivacaine, with adrenaline
  • RCOG: bupivacaine is the gold standard for obstetric epidural analgesia
  • Epidural space: semiliquid fat, nerve roots pass through
  • Acts on nerve roots (epidural + diffuses subarachnoid); some leaks out for paravertebral blocks

(i) Thoracic

Narrow space, small volume

Wide band of mid and lower thoracic analgesia. Pain after thoracic or upper abdominal surgery; catheter for days.

(ii) Lumbar

Wide space, large volume

Lower abdomen, pelvis, legs. Uses like spinal.

(iii) Caudal

Through the sacral hiatus

Pelvis and perineum. Vaginal delivery, anorectal and genitourinary operations.

Chapter 27 · Actions and adverse drug reactions

Actions and ADRs

Every LA is eventually absorbed. Blood level decides the CNS and heart effects.

Local actions

  • Little or no local irritation (clinical LAs)
  • Block non-selectively: sensory endings, nerve trunks, neuromuscular junction, ganglionic synapse, receptors
  • Myelinated fibres more susceptible · small fibres > large
  • ↓ ACh release from motor nerve endings
  • Around a mixed nerve: skin anaesthesia + paralysis of the muscles it supplies

Systemic actions: CNS

Basic action
All LAs = neuronal inhibition
Why stimulation first
Inhibitory neurones are inhibited first
High dose
All neurones inhibited → flat EEG
Synthetic LAs
Much less CNS-active than cocaine · little effect at safe doses · symptoms of overdose: see ADRs below

Precautions and interactions

1. Aspirate lightly before injecting → avoid intravascular injection

↓

4. Avoid adrenaline-containing LA in the patients below
Ischaemic heart disease, Cardiac arrhythmia, Thyrotoxicosis, Uncontrolled hypertension, On β-blockers, On tricyclic antidepressants

  • Tricyclics: block adrenaline uptake → potentiate it
