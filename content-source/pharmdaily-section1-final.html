Why sublingual is fast

Bypassing First-Pass Metabolism: When a substance is swallowed, it must pass through the hostile environment of the stomach and intestines, and then travel via the portal vein directly to the liver. The liver heavily filters and deactivates a large portion of the compound before it ever reaches the rest of the body—a process known as the first-pass effect. Sublingual delivery completely bypasses this loop, draining directly into the internal jugular and facial veins to provide an immediate effect.

Metered dose inhalers: advantages

  • Small and easy to carry
  • Acts quickly and is easy to use
  • Contents can't get contaminated
  • Dose can be controlled

Metered dose inhalers: disadvantages

  • Fairly costly
  • Can irritate the airway locally
  • Pressing and breathing in must be timed together; poor timing means too little drug reaches the lungs
  • Much of the dose is wasted, settling on the mouth and throat lining

New drug delivery systems

Aim: get the drug to its target at the right rate and concentration, for the most benefit and the fewest adverse effects.

Why use them

  • Better compliance
  • Old drugs re-patented in new forms

Drawback

  • Very expensive

Bioavailability: the rate and extent to which a dose reaches the systemic circulation unchanged. IV = 100%. Same amount of drug but different blood levels = bioinequivalent. Matters most for narrow-margin drugs (digoxin) and those needing precise dosing (oral anticoagulants, hypoglycaemics).

Distribution

Depends on lipid solubility, pH, plasma protein binding, tissue transporters and regional blood flow. Limited by the blood–brain, blood–CSF and placental barriers. Only lipid-soluble drugs enter the brain; dopamine can't, but levodopa can.

Apparent volume of distribution (V) = IV dose ÷ plasma concentration.
Low V: drug held in plasma (warfarin, diclofenac ~0.15 L/kg). High V: drug stored in tissues (digoxin 6 L/kg), so haemodialysis can't remove it in poisoning.

Redistribution

Highly lipid-soluble drugs first reach well-perfused organs (brain, heart, kidney), then shift to bulky muscle and fat. That ends their action fast. The same explains why oral diazepam works as a short hypnotic despite a half-life over 30 hours. Repeated doses fill the fat stores and the drug becomes long-acting.

Metabolism (Biotransformation)

Turns lipid-soluble drugs into water-soluble ones so the kidney can excrete them. Mainly in the liver; also kidney, gut, lungs and plasma. Hydrophilic drugs (streptomycin, neostigmine) are excreted mostly unchanged.

Phase I and Phase II

Phase I · functionalisationAdds or exposes a reactive group.
  • Oxidation, CYP450: hydroxylation (warfarin, phenytoin, ibuprofen), dealkylation (morphine, codeine), deamination (diazepam), desulfuration (thiopentone), N-oxidation (paracetamol)
  • Oxidation, non-CYP: MAO (dopamine, adrenaline), xanthine oxidase (6-mercaptopurine)
  • Reduction: chloramphenicol, clonazepam, methadone; KDT adds chloral hydrate, halothane, warfarin
  • Hydrolysis: esters (procaine, succinylcholine), amides (lidocaine, procainamide); also aspirin, pethidine, oxytocin
  • Cyclisation: a straight-chain compound closes into a ring. Proguanil → cycloguanil
  • Decyclisation: barbiturates, phenytoin
Phase II · conjugationJoins a body substance so the drug becomes water-soluble. Products are mostly inactive; exceptions are morphine glucuronide and minoxidil sulphate.
  • Glucuronide (the most important): chloramphenicol, morphine, lorazepam, diazepam, aspirin, paracetamol, metronidazole, digoxin
  • Sulphate: paracetamol, warfarin, methyldopa, chloramphenicol, steroids
  • Glutathione: paracetamol (fails in overdose → liver damage)
  • Glycine: salicylic acid
  • Acetylation: isoniazid, dapsone, hydralazine, sulfonamides, procainamide (slow and fast acetylators)
  • Methylation: adrenaline, dopamine, histamine, methyldopa, captopril
For your clinicEster local anaesthetics (procaine) are broken down by plasma esterases; amides (lidocaine) by the liver.

  • Loading dose depends on volume of distribution; maintenance dose on clearance

Chapter 4 · Pharmacodynamics

What the drug does to the body

Drugs don't give cells new jobs. They speed up or slow down what cells already do (gene-based drugs are the exception).

Ways drugs act

Stimulation
Adrenaline on the heart, pilocarpine on salivary glands. Too much can tip into depression (picrotoxin: convulsions, then coma).
Depression
Barbiturates on CNS, quinidine on heart, omeprazole on acid secretion
Irritation
Non-selective, often harmful, on less specialised cells
Replacement
Natural metabolites or hormones in deficiency: levodopa in parkinsonism, insulin in diabetes, iron in anaemia
Anti-infective
Kills or stops microbes
Cytotoxic
Kills cancer cells
Immune modification
Vaccines, immunosuppressants such as cyclosporine

A few act by a physical or chemical property: ispaghula (bulk), petroleum jelly (lubricant), activated charcoal (adsorption), mannitol (osmosis), antacids (neutralise acid), chelators like EDTA and dimercaprol (bind metals), cholestyramine (binds bile acids). Most bind a protein target, which is what makes them selective.

Receptors, Enzymes, Ion channels, Transporters

Enzymes

Most drugs that act on enzymes inhibit them. The inhibition is either competitive or non-competitive.

Competitive (equilibrium type): the drug is structurally similar to the normal substrate and competes with it for the enzyme's catalytic site. No product forms, or a non-functional one does. A new equilibrium is reached with the drug present. Km rises, Vmax is unchanged: more substrate is needed for half-maximal velocity, but enough substrate displaces the inhibitor and the same maximum velocity is reached.

Transporters

Transporters (carriers) are membrane proteins that move substrates across membranes. They either allow facilitated diffusion down the concentration gradient or pump a metabolite or ion against the gradient using metabolic energy. Many drugs bind the solute carrier (SLC) class of transporters and block the normal transport of a metabolite or ion.

Receptors

Receptor: a macromolecule, or a binding site on it, located on the surface or inside the effector cell. It recognises the signal molecule or drug and starts the response, but has no other function of its own.

Most drugs don't bind the effectors (enzymes, channels, transporters, structural proteins) directly. They act through specific regulatory macromolecules that control these effectors. Those macromolecules, or the sites on them that bind the drug, are the receptors.

Key terms

Ligand
Any molecule that attaches selectively to a particular receptor or site. The term only means affinity (ability to bind), regardless of any functional change. Agonists and competitive antagonists are both ligands of the same receptor.
Agonist
Activates a receptor to produce an effect similar to that of the physiological signal molecule
Inverse agonist
Activates a receptor to produce an effect opposite to that of the agonist
Partial agonist
Activates a receptor to produce a submaximal effect, but antagonises the action of a full agonist
Antagonist
Prevents the action of an agonist on a receptor, or the response that follows, but has no effect of its own

Receptor occupation theory

Clark (1937): drug (D) and receptor (R) interact by the law of mass action, and the effect (E) is a direct function of the drug–receptor complex (DR) formed.

D + R ⇌ DR → E

Ariëns and Stephenson: occupying the receptor is essential but not enough. The agonist must also cause a conformational change in the receptor. A stimulus (S), stronger or weaker depending on the agonist, comes between the complex and the effect.

D + R ⇌ DR → S → E

Two-state model

A receptor flips between inactive (Ri) and active (Ra). A drug shifts the balance by which state it prefers.

Affinity and intrinsic activity

AffinityThe ability of a drug to bind the receptor. It sets the concentration of drug needed around the receptor to form a given number of drug–receptor complexes. Higher affinity: the same number of complexes forms at a lower concentration.
Intrinsic activity (IA) or efficacyThe capacity of a drug to cause a functional change in the receptor. It ranges from 0 (nil) to 1 (maximal). Affinity and intrinsic activity are independent properties.
0 to −1Inverse agonist
0Antagonist
0 – 1Partial
+1Full agonist
Agonist · IA = 1
Affinity and maximal intrinsic activity. Adrenaline (adrenergic receptors), morphine (μ opioid receptors).
Competitive antagonist · IA = 0
Affinity but no intrinsic activity. Atropine (muscarinic receptors), naloxone (μ opioid receptors).
Partial agonist · IA 0 to 1
Affinity and submaximal intrinsic activity. Pindolol (β receptors), buprenorphine (μ opioid receptors).
Inverse agonist · IA 0 to −1
Affinity but intrinsic activity with a minus sign. Seen only with receptors showing constitutive activation: partly active even with no agonist (tonically active), e.g. the benzodiazepine receptor.
Why a partial agonist has a lower ceiling
  • Not because of low affinity. Some partial agonists bind more strongly and are more potent dose-for-dose than the full agonist.
  • Buprenorphine is 25 times more potent than morphine (full μ agonist), but as a partial agonist it can't relieve very severe pain or deeply depress respiration.
  • It antagonises a full agonist: it occupies many receptors while giving a small response, leaving fewer for the full agonist.
  • So buprenorphine precipitates withdrawal in a highly morphine-dependent person, but can substitute for morphine at lower levels of dependence.
Spare receptorsMany full agonists give a maximum response while occupying under 1% of receptors. The rest are a reserve.
Silent receptorsSites that bind a drug but give no response, like plasma proteins. Better called drug acceptors or "sites of loss".

1. G-protein coupled receptors (GPCR)

The agonist binds the receptor on the cell surface and changes its shape, so the G-protein can bind. The G-protein takes up GTP in place of GDP, and its α subunit separates from the βγ dimer to act on an effector. There are three main pathways.

(a) Adenylyl cyclase: cAMP pathway

Adrenaline binds the β-adrenergic receptor
↓
Stimulatory G-protein (Gs) binds GTP; Gsα-GTP separates
↓
Gsα activates adenylyl cyclase (inner side of the membrane)
↓
ATP → cAMP (second messenger)
↓
cAMP activates protein kinase A (PKA)
↓
PKA phosphorylates troponin (stronger contraction) and phospholamban (faster Ca²⁺ uptake into SR, so faster relaxation)
Also via cAMPPKA phosphorylates phosphorylase kinase, which activates phosphorylase: glycogen breaks down for energy. Ca²⁺ also enters through membrane Ca²⁺ channels activated by Gsα and PKA.
Opposite: inhibitory GiACh on the muscarinic M₂ receptor activates Gi. Giα inhibits adenylyl cyclase. The βγ dimer of Gi opens K⁺ channels: hyperpolarisation, slower impulse generation.

(b) Phospholipase C: IP₃–DAG pathway

Agonist (e.g. histamine on H₁) activates Gq
↓
Gqα-GTP activates phospholipase Cβ (PLCβ)
↓
PLCβ splits membrane PIP₂ into IP₃ + DAG
IP₃Water-soluble. Moves into the cytosol and releases Ca²⁺ from the endoplasmic reticulum.
DAGLipophilic, stays in the membrane. With Ca²⁺, it activates protein kinase C (PKC), which phosphorylates many intracellular proteins.
Ca²⁺: the third messenger
  • With calmodulin (CAM), activates myosin light chain kinase (MLCK): smooth muscle contraction
  • Also activates calcium-calmodulin protein kinase (CCPK) and other effectors
  • Mediates contraction, glandular secretion, transmitter release, eicosanoid synthesis, neuronal excitability, cell proliferation
  • Cytosolic Ca²⁺ is kept very low (~100 nM) by pumps; it is taken back into the ER and pumped out of the cell
Signal ends whenIP₃ is dephosphorylated to inositol (reused to make PIP₂); DAG is converted back to phospholipid or deacylated to arachidonic acid.

(c) Direct channel regulation

Activated G-proteins (Gs, Gi, Go) can open or close Ca²⁺ and K⁺ channels without any second messenger, causing hyperpolarisation, depolarisation or a change in cell Ca²⁺. This is mostly done by the βγ dimer.

Gs
Opens Ca²⁺ channels in myocardium and skeletal muscle
Gi, Go
Open K⁺ channels in heart and smooth muscle; inhibit neuronal Ca²⁺ channels

2. Ion channel receptors (ligand-gated)

Cell-surface receptors that enclose an ion-selective channel (for Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺ or Cl⁻) within their own molecule. Agonist binding opens the channel and causes depolarisation, hyperpolarisation or a change in ionic composition, depending on the ion.
Nicotinic cholinergic, GABA-A, Glycine (inhibitory), Glutamate (excitatory), 5-HT₃
  • The nicotinic receptor is a pentamer: subunits arranged like a rosette around the channel; the α subunits carry the agonist binding sites
  • Some have secondary (allosteric) sites: the benzodiazepine receptor modulates the GABA-A gated Cl⁻ channel
  • No coupling protein or second messenger, so onset and offset are the fastest (milliseconds)

3. Transmembrane enzyme-linked receptors

Used mainly by peptide hormones. A large extracellular ligand-binding domain is connected, through a single transmembrane helix, to an intracellular subunit with enzyme activity: usually a protein kinase, sometimes guanylyl cyclase.

↓
Phosphorylation cascade: metabolism, cell growth, differentiation

Unlike RTKs, these have no enzyme (catalytic) domain of their own. Agonist-induced dimerisation makes the intracellular domain bind a free-moving cytosolic tyrosine kinase, JAK (Janus kinase).

5. Receptors regulating gene expression (nuclear receptors)

Intracellular (cytoplasmic or nuclear) soluble proteins that respond to lipid-soluble chemical messengers entering the cell. Each binds specific genes once activated.
Lipid-soluble hormone crosses the cell membrane
↓
Binds its receptor, which is held inactive by HSP-90
↓
HSP-90 is released; the receptor dimerises and its DNA-binding region folds into an active shape
↓
Dimer moves to the nucleus and binds co-activator or co-repressor proteins
↓
Attaches to hormone response elements on DNA (GREs for glucocorticoids)
↓
Gene expression promoted or repressed → mRNA → ribosome → specific proteins → cell function changes
Glucocorticoids, Mineralocorticoids, Androgens, Estrogens, Progesterone, Thyroxine, Vitamin D, Vitamin A
Glucocorticoid receptor (GR) structure
  • Steroid-binding domain near the carboxy terminus
  • DNA-binding domain in the middle, with two zinc fingers
  • The two are joined by a hinge region
  • Each steroid hormone binds its own receptor and directs a unique pattern of protein synthesis, so each acts on different target cells
  • Different ligands of the same receptor can act differently in different tissues: SERMs (tamoxifen, raloxifene) on estrogen target organs
  • Slowest mechanism (hours): new protein must be made first. Effects outlast the hormone because these proteins turn over slowly.

Regulation of receptors

Receptors are dynamic. Their number (density) and efficacy change with the level of ongoing activity, feedback from their own signal output, and other physiological or disease influences.

Physiological example
Estrogens increase oxytocin receptors on the myometrium, so the uterus becomes progressively more sensitive to oxytocin in the third trimester, especially near term
Supersensitivity (up-regulation)
Prolonged lack of agonist (denervation, or continued use of an antagonist or a drug that reduces input) makes the receptor and effector system supersensitive. Mechanism: unmasking or proliferation of receptors, or more signal amplification.
Clinical relevance
Withdrawal syndromes after clonidine, CNS depressants or opioids; sudden stopping of propranolol in angina pectoris
Desensitisation (refractoriness)
Continued agonist stimulation makes the receptor less responsive. Either masking or internalisation of the receptor (develops and fades quickly), or decreased receptor synthesis, i.e. down-regulation (develops slowly, over weeks).

Dose and response

Response rises with dose, then levels off. On a log-dose scale it becomes an S-curve.

A: referenceB: less potent, same efficacyC: same potency, less efficacy
PotencyHow much drug you need for an effect. Curve position: further right = less potent. Compared by EC50; depends on affinity.
Efficacy (Emax)The maximum response possible. Curve height. Clinically matters more than potency.
Pethidine vs morphine
Less potent, equally efficacious analgesic
Aspirin vs morphine
Less potent and less efficacious
Furosemide vs metolazone
Less potent but more efficacious diuretic
Diazepam vs pentobarbitone
More potent but less efficacious, which makes diazepam safer in overdose
Therapeutic index = LD50 / ED50. An estimate of safety from animal studies. In patients, the useful idea is the therapeutic window: between the lowest effective dose and the highest dose with acceptable side effects.

A steep curve means doses need individualising (hydralazine); a flat one means standard doses suit most people (hydrochlorothiazide).

Combinations

Synergism

One drug increases the action of the other.

Additive: A + B = A + B
  • Aspirin + paracetamol (analgesic)
  • Nitrous oxide + halothane (anaesthetic)
  • Amlodipine + atenolol (antihypertensive)
  • Glibenclamide + metformin (hypoglycaemic)
  • Ephedrine + theophylline (bronchodilator)
Supra-additive: A + B > A + B
  • ACh + physostigmine (blocks breakdown)
  • Levodopa + carbidopa (blocks peripheral metabolism)
  • Adrenaline + cocaine or desipramine (blocks neuronal uptake)
  • Sulfamethoxazole + trimethoprim (sequential folate block)
  • Enalapril + hydrochlorothiazide (two causes of high BP)
  • Tyramine + MAO inhibitors
For your clinicLocal anaesthetics work by blocking Na⁺ channels in nerves, so the pain signal never fires. That's drug action on an ion channel, not a receptor.

Antagonism

One drug reduces or abolishes the other: A + B < A + B.

Physical
Activated charcoal adsorbs alkaloids in poisoning
Chemical
KMnO₄ oxidises alkaloids (gastric lavage). Tannins precipitate alkaloids. Chelators (BAL, calcium disodium edetate) bind arsenic, lead. Nitrites in cyanide poisoning. Some drugs react in the same syringe: thiopentone or penicillin G with succinylcholine; heparin with penicillin.
Physiological
Opposite actions via different receptors: histamine vs adrenaline on bronchi and BP; hydrochlorothiazide vs triamterene on urinary K⁺; insulin vs glucagon on blood sugar
Pharmacological
At the same receptor: competitive or non-competitive
Competitive
  • Same site as the agonist
  • Resembles the agonist
  • Parallel right shift of the curve
  • Same Emax: more agonist overcomes it
  • Response depends on both concentrations
  • ACh vs atropine, morphine vs naloxone
Non-competitive
  • Another (allosteric) site
  • Doesn't resemble the agonist
  • Flattens the curve
  • Lower Emax: more agonist doesn't help
  • Diazepam vs bicuculline
  • Not used clinically
Non-equilibrium (irreversible)Binds the same receptor so tightly (covalent bond) that agonist can't displace it. Curve shifts right and Emax falls. KD Tripathi calls this a type of non-competitive antagonism. Example: phenoxybenzamine against adrenaline at α receptors.
