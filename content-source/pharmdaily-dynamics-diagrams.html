What the drug does to the body

Drugs don't give cells new jobs. They speed up or slow down what cells already do. Every idea below starts with a picture.

Ways drugs act

Stimulation→Adrenaline (heart), pilocarpine (salivary glands)Too much can tip into depression: picrotoxin, convulsions then coma
Depression→Barbiturates (CNS), quinidine (heart), omeprazole (acid)
Irritation→Non-selective, often harmfulOn less specialised cells
Replacement→Levodopa, insulin, ironParkinsonism, diabetes, anaemia
Anti-infective→Kills or stops microbes
Cytotoxic→Kills cancer cells

Transporters KDT

DefinitionCarrier proteins that move substrates across membranes: by facilitated diffusion down the gradient, or by pumping against it with metabolic energy. Many drugs bind the solute carrier (SLC) class and block the normal transport of a metabolite or ion.

In the nerve ending: reuptake blockers

In the kidney: diuretics and probenecid

Receptors

Definition KDTReceptor: a macromolecule, or a binding site on it, located on the surface or inside the effector cell. It recognises the signal molecule or drug and starts the response, but has no other function of its own.

Most drugs don't bind effectors (enzymes, channels, transporters, structural proteins) directly. They act through the regulatory macromolecules that control them: the receptors.

Receptor occupation theory KDT

AffinityThe ability to bind the receptor. It sets the concentration of drug needed to form a given number of drug–receptor complexes. Higher affinity: the same number forms at a lower concentration.
Intrinsic activity (efficacy)The capacity to cause a functional change in the receptor, from 0 (nil) to 1 (maximal). Independent of affinity.

Key terms, by the response they give

LigandAny molecule that attaches selectively to a particular receptor or site. Means only affinity, regardless of functional change. Agonists and competitive antagonists are both ligands of the same receptor.
AgonistActivates a receptor to produce an effect similar to that of the physiological signal molecule.
Partial agonistActivates a receptor to produce a submaximal effect, but antagonises the action of a full agonist.
AntagonistPrevents the action of an agonist on a receptor, or the response that follows, but has no effect of its own.
Inverse agonistActivates a receptor to produce an effect opposite to that of the agonist. Seen only with constitutively active receptors, e.g. the benzodiazepine receptor.

Two-state model KDT

Why a partial agonist has a lower ceiling KDT

It also blocks the full agonistIt occupies many receptors while giving a small response, leaving fewer for morphine. So buprenorphine precipitates withdrawal in a highly morphine-dependent person, but can substitute at lower levels of dependence.
Spare receptors KDTMany full agonists give a maximal response while occupying under 1% of receptors. The rest are a reserve.
Silent receptors KDTSites that bind a drug but give no response, like plasma proteins. Better called drug acceptors or "sites of loss".

Regulation of receptors KDT

Supersensitivity (up-regulation)→Prolonged lack of agonist: denervation, or an antagonist or a drug that reduces input. Unmasking or proliferation of receptors, or more amplification.
Clinical→Clonidine, CNS depressant, opioid withdrawal; stopping propranolol suddenly in angina
Desensitisation→Continued agonist: masking or internalisation (fast, fades fast) or decreased synthesis, i.e. down-regulation (slow, over weeks)
Physiological→Estrogens raise oxytocin receptors on the myometrium, so the uterus grows more sensitive in the third trimester, especially near term

Dose and response

Pethidine vs morphine→Less potent, equally efficacious
Aspirin vs morphine→Less potent, less efficacious
Furosemide vs metolazone→Less potent, more efficacious
Diazepam vs pentobarbitone→More potent, less efficaciousSo diazepam is safer in overdose

Kinds of antagonism

Physical→Activated charcoal adsorbs alkaloids
Chemical→KMnO₄, tannins, chelators (BAL, Ca disodium edetate), nitrites in cyanide poisoningSame-syringe reactions: thiopentone or penicillin G with succinylcholine; heparin with penicillin
Physiological KDT→Histamine vs adrenaline; insulin vs glucagonOpposite actions through different receptors; also hydrochlorothiazide vs triamterene on K⁺
Pharmacological→At the same receptor: competitive or non-competitive
Non-equilibrium (irreversible)Binds the same receptor so tightly (covalent bond) that the agonist can't displace it. Curve shifts right and Emax falls. KD Tripathi counts it as a type of non-competitive antagonism. Example: phenoxybenzamine against adrenaline at α receptors.
