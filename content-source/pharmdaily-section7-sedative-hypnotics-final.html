Chapter 29 · Sedative-hypnotics

Calming down and falling asleep

What sedatives and hypnotics are, how normal sleep is built, and the four groups of drugs used for insomnia.

Two definitions

Sedative: a drug that reduces excitement and anxiety without inducing sleep. It decreases responsiveness and motor activity. Some drowsiness may occur.
Hypnotic: a drug that induces and/or maintains sleep similar to normal arousable sleep.

"Hypnotic" here has nothing to do with hypnosis, the trance-like state where a person is under another's control.

Where the drugs differ
  • Quick onset, short action, steep dose-response curve: better as hypnotics
  • Slow onset, flatter curve: better as sedatives
  • Barbiturates walk the whole ladder. Benzodiazepines have a low ceiling: they don't reach anaesthesia or coma when given orally
  • Most important use of this class: treatment of insomnia

Normal sleep

Sleep runs in cycles of about 80–100 minutes: stages 0 → 4, then REM, repeated through the night.

Stage 0 · awake1–2%
Lying down until sleep, plus short night awakenings. EEG: α waves (eyes closed), β waves (eyes open).
Stage 1 · dozing3–6%
α waves mixed with θ waves. Eye movements fewer; neck muscles relax.
Stage 2 · unequivocal sleep40–50%
θ waves with sleep spindles; K complexes on sensory stimulation. Easily aroused.
Stage 3 · deep sleep transition5–8%
θ and δ waves, spindles. Not easily aroused.
Stage 4 · cerebral sleep10–20%
δ waves dominate. Hard to arouse. Night terrors happen here.
REM · paradoxical sleep20–30%
Darting eye movements, dreams and nightmares. Heart rate, BP and breathing irregular; muscles fully relaxed.
Slow-wave sleep (SWS) = stages 3 + 4 together. Stages 1–4 are non-REM (NREM) sleep.
α waves
High amplitude, 8–14 cycles/s
β waves
Low amplitude, 15–35 cycles/s
θ waves
Low amplitude, 4–7 cycles/s
δ waves
High amplitude, 0.5–3 cycles/s

Chapter 29 · Benzodiazepines

Benzodiazepines

Selective CNS depressants: they sedate, relieve anxiety, help sleep, stop seizures and relax muscle. The most used group, and the safest.

Benzodiazepines have a lower ceiling of CNS depression than barbiturates and a high therapeutic index. Even 10 hypnotic doses taken together don't usually endanger life: the patient can be roused and breathing rarely needs support.

1. Mechanism of action

Benzodiazepine binds the GABA-A – benzodiazepine receptor – Cl⁻ channel complex

Benzodiazepines bind specific subunits of the GABA-A receptor, which is why one group gives sedative-hypnotic, anticonvulsant, antianxiety and muscle relaxant actions.

Inside the receptor

No GABA-mimetic action
Benzodiazepines don't open the Cl⁻ channel by themselves; they only help GABA. That explains their lower ceiling.
α1 subunit
Sedative, hypnotic, amnesic actions. Z compounds act selectively here.
α2 subunit
Anxiolytic and muscle relaxant actions. Diazepam acts on α1, α2, α3 and α5: broad spectrum.
Where in the brain
Midbrain reticular formation (wakefulness) and limbic system. Muscle relaxation: medulla. Ataxia: cerebellum.

Benzodiazepine vs barbiturate at the same channel

BenzodiazepineBinds benzodiazepine site↓↑ frequency of opening↓Needs GABAlow ceiling, safe
BarbiturateBinds its own site↓↑ duration of opening↓High dose opens it aloneno ceiling, coma

What this does: actions

Antianxiety
Relatively selective; not just a side effect of sedation. Drowsiness wears off with regular use, but anxiety relief stays.
Muscle relaxant
Centrally acting, without impairing voluntary activity. Clonazepam and diazepam are strongest.
Anticonvulsant
Clonazepam, diazepam, nitrazepam, lorazepam and flurazepam are strongest. Tolerance limits long-term use in epilepsy.
Other
IV diazepam causes some analgesia (barbiturates cause hyperalgesia). Diazepam lowers night-time gastric acid and prevents stress ulcers.

3. Insomnia: effect on sleep

They give a sense of deep, refreshing sleep by:

They shorten each REM phase but allow more REM cycles, so total REM falls less than with barbiturates. Some tolerance to the sleep effect develops with nightly use.

4. Adverse effects

Drug dependence
Stopping suddenly after long use causes withdrawal: rebound insomnia, dysphoria, irritability, bad dreams, tremors, restlessness, anxiety. Taper the dose when stopping.
CNS
Drowsiness, light-headedness, anterograde amnesia, lassitude, hangover, confusion. Impaired judgement, motor skills and cognition: patients shouldn't drive.
Other
Muscle weakness, blurred vision, vertigo, ataxia
Also
Sleep walking and sleep driving with no memory of it · Paradoxical stimulation, nightmares, sweating (esp. flurazepam) · Elderly more prone to psychomotor effects · Aggravate sleep apnoea · Dry mouth, urinary incontinence
Pregnancy
Floppy infant syndrome: given in labour, benzodiazepines cause flaccidity and respiratory depression in the newborn. Flumazenil reverses it.
Cleft lip and palate: reported with first-trimester use.
Abuse
Low dependence liability: weak reinforcers, seldom abused alone. Withdrawal usually mild; worse with fast-eliminated drugs. Convulsions rare.

Drug interactions

Other CNS depressants
Alcohol and other CNS depressants add up: excessive impairment
Sodium valproate
Together they have caused psychotic symptoms
CYP3A4 inhibitors
Ketoconazole, erythromycin prolong benzodiazepine action. Cimetidine, isoniazid and oral contraceptives also slow benzodiazepine metabolism.
For your clinicA patient on erythromycin given oral midazolam for sedation stays drowsy much longer: erythromycin slows its breakdown by CYP3A4.

5. Antidote: flumazenil

A benzodiazepine analogue with almost no action of its own. It competes with benzodiazepines (and inverse agonists) at the benzodiazepine receptor and reverses their effects. It also blocks zolpidem-like Z compounds.
Route
Intravenous. Acts in seconds, lasts 1–2 hours; t½ about 1 hour.
Reverse benzodiazepine anaesthesia
Patient wakes and regains control within a minute of 0.3–1 mg IV. Re-sedation may come within an hour.
Benzodiazepine overdose
0.2 mg/min IV until conscious, max 1 mg; most respond within 5 min. Supportive care alone works for most.
Hepatic encephalopathy
Used to reverse encephalopathy in liver failure
Caution
Risky in mixed overdose with a tricyclic antidepressant, and in benzodiazepine-dependent people: can trigger severe withdrawal and seizures
Side effects
Agitation, discomfort, fear, anxiety, coldness, withdrawal seizures
For your clinicKeep flumazenil ready whenever you give IV midazolam or diazepam for dental sedation. It reverses over-sedation in about a minute.

More to know: advantages over barbiturates

Safety
Wide therapeutic index; risk of fatal CNS depression is minimal
Sleep quality
Deep, refreshing sleep; less distortion of sleep stages; less rebound on stopping
Breathing and heart
Don't affect respiratory or cardiovascular function at hypnotic doses. Higher doses cause mild respiratory depression and low BP, which matters only in lung or heart disease. IV injection lowers BP.
Enzymes
Don't induce liver microsomal enzymes, so no enzyme-induction interactions
Abuse
Lower abuse liability; tolerance mild; dependence and withdrawal less marked
Antidote
Specific antagonist flumazenil is available

Pharmacokinetics

  • Lipid solubility differs more than 50-fold between benzodiazepines, which drives most of the differences
  • IM absorption is irregular, except lorazepam
  • Plasma protein binding: flurazepam 10% to diazepam 99%
  • Lipid-soluble ones enter brain fast, then redistribute: a single dose can act briefly even with a long t½
  • Metabolised in liver by CYP3A4 and CYP2C19; many have active metabolites, so the real effect outlasts plasma t½
  • Diazepam undergoes enterohepatic circulation; most are excreted in urine as glucuronides
  • Cross the placenta and enter milk

Individual drugs

Diazepam
Oldest, all-purpose benzodiazepine. Active metabolites (desmethyldiazepam, oxazepam). Less rebound insomnia on stopping.
Flurazepam
Very long t½: next-morning sedation, builds up over 3–5 days. Not for the elderly.
Nitrazepam
Equipotent to diazepam. For frequent night awakenings when some daytime sedation is acceptable.
Lorazepam
No active metabolite, so no build-up. Mainly for anxiety; also sleep in tense patients.
Alprazolam
Mainly for anxiety; also a night-time hypnotic. Rapid absorption, short t½, few next-day effects.
Triazolam
Very potent, peaks in under an hour. Early-morning waking with anxiety; anterograde amnesia. Withdrawn in the UK.
Chapter 29 · Other hypnotics

Z compounds, melatonin, orexin blockers, barbiturates

Newer drugs built to fix the benzodiazepines' weak points, and the old barbiturates they replaced.

Z compounds (non-benzodiazepine hypnotics)

Mechanism: agonists at the benzodiazepine site of the GABA-A receptor. Chemically different from benzodiazepines, but they act on a subset of benzodiazepine receptors, selectively those with the α1 subunit. Flumazenil blocks them.

Why they're preferred for insomnia

  • Strong hypnotic-amnesic action
  • Weak antianxiety, muscle relaxant and anticonvulsant action
  • Lower abuse potential than hypnotic benzodiazepines
  • Shorter action: fewer next-day effects

Still remember

  • Short-term use only (mostly 1–4 weeks)
  • Some morning impairment possible
  • Overdose safety is like benzodiazepines
Zopiclone
First non-benzodiazepine hypnotic. Oral; for insomnia. ADR: metallic taste, dry mouth. T½ 5–6 h; doesn't alter REM; used to wean insomniacs off regular benzodiazepines; also bitter after-taste, impaired judgement, milder dependence.
Eszopiclone
Active S-enantiomer of zopiclone. Lengthens stage 2; little effect on sleep architecture. No active metabolite, t½ 6 h, little tolerance: for short-term and chronic insomnia.
Zolpidem
α1-selective, strong hypnotic. Little effect on slow-wave sleep; little fading with nightly use; low abuse potential. t½ 2 h. For sleep-onset insomnia and night awakenings, 1–2 weeks. One of the most prescribed hypnotics.
Zaleplon
Shortest acting (t½ 1 h; 30% bioavailability due to first-pass). For sleep-onset insomnia; can be taken late at night without morning sedation. Doesn't stop night awakenings.
Etizolam
Benzodiazepine analogue with the full range of benzodiazepine actions; flumazenil reverses it. Abuse potential: a controlled drug in many countries. For short-term anxiety and sleep disorder.

Melatonin group

Melatonin
Hormone of the pineal gland, secreted at night. Binds MT1 and MT2 receptors in the brain. Shortens time to fall asleep. Oral; used in jet lag and insomnia in the elderly. MT1 and MT2 are GPCRs; it sets the circadian rhythm. Low doses (2–10 mg) don't depress the CNS. Also for shift workers; not a dependable hypnotic.
Ramelteon
Selective MT1, MT2 agonist; more pronounced hypnotic effect than melatonin. Oral; useful for sleep-onset difficulty. No GABA action, no next-morning sedation, no rebound or dependence. 8 mg half an hour before bed. Low bioavailability (first-pass), t½ 1–3 h. Fluvoxamine, ciprofloxacin and fluconazole raise its levels; rifampicin lowers them. ADR: fatigue, sleepiness, dizziness.
Tasimelteon
Melatonin receptor agonist
Agomelatine
MT1, MT2 agonist and 5-HT2C antagonist. Improves sleep quality; antidepressant effect comparable to paroxetine and venlafaxine. Liver enzymes may rise.

Barbiturates

Prototype global CNS depressants, depressing all excitable cells. Popular until the 1960s; not used now to induce sleep or calm patients.
Mechanism
Potentiate GABA by increasing the duration of Cl⁻ channel opening. At high doses they open the channel directly (GABA-mimetic), block glutamate AMPA receptors and depress Na⁺ and K⁺ channels: hence deep CNS depression.
CNS
Dose-dependent: sedation → sleep → anaesthesia → coma. Cut REM and stages 3, 4; hangover next morning. Impair memory and judgement. Phenobarbitone has selective anticonvulsant action.
Other actions
Higher doses depress breathing, lower BP and cardiac contractility. Toxic doses: respiratory failure, cardiovascular collapse.
Pharmacokinetics
Thiopentone (highly lipid-soluble) enters brain instantly, then redistributes: wakes in 6–10 min. Phenobarbitone is excreted unchanged in urine, t½ 80–120 h; alkalinising urine speeds its excretion.
Interactions
Induce liver enzymes (CYP3A4, 2D6, 2C9, UGT): speed up their own and other drugs' metabolism (warfarin, steroids, theophylline). Add to other CNS depressants.
Adverse effects
Hangover, confusion. Tolerance and dependence; high abuse liability. Withdrawal: excitement, hallucinations, delirium, convulsions; deaths have occurred.

Therapeutic uses

Anticonvulsant
Status epilepticus: phenobarbitone, thiopental sodium · Generalised tonic-clonic and partial seizures: phenobarbitone
IV anaesthetic (induction)
Thiopental sodium (most commonly used), methohexital sodium
Kernicterus
Phenobarbitone induces the liver enzymes that metabolise bilirubin: used for kernicterus and hyperbilirubinaemia in the newborn

Acute barbiturate poisoning

Flabby, comatose patient with shallow failing breathing, falling BP, cardiovascular collapse, renal shutdown. No specific antidote.

Gastric lavage; leave activated charcoal in the stomach

Choosing a hypnotic

Transient insomnia1–3 days
New place, noise, travel, jet lag, shift work. Use a rapidly eliminated hypnotic to avoid next-morning effects.
Short-term3–21 days
Stress, bereavement, illness. Low dose of a drug matched to the problem, for 2–3 weeks max. Anxiety prominent: a benzodiazepine whose action lasts into the day. Elderly: short acting.
Chronicover 3 weeks
Non-drug measures first: exercise, sleep hygiene, cut coffee and alcohol, treat the cause. Intermittent hypnotic (once every 3 days) in selected cases.
Rules worth an exam line
  • Any hypnotic except zolpidem-like drugs stops working after a few days of regular use
  • Next-morning impairment comes from long action (hangover) or short action (rebound anxiety)
  • All hypnotics worsen obstructive sleep apnoea: contraindicated
  • The patient's own feeling of sleeping well and waking fresh matters most
