Oral

Why it's the default

  • Safe and convenient
  • Cheap, needs no trained person
  • Painless, self-administered

Where it fails

  • Slow onset: not for emergencies
  • Unconscious, uncooperative or vomiting patients
  • Some aren't absorbed (streptomycin)
  • Destroyed by gut juices: penicillin G, insulin
  • Destroyed in the liver: GTN, testosterone, lidocaine
  • Bad taste (chloramphenicol), nausea (emetine)
Local effect in the gutnot absorbed
Antacids, Activated charcoal, Neomycin
Systemic effect
Most tablets and capsules: absorbed, then act elsewhere.

Why sublingual is fast

Bypassing First-Pass Metabolism: When a substance is swallowed, it must pass through the hostile environment of the stomach and intestines, and then travel via the portal vein directly to the liver. The liver heavily filters and deactivates a large portion of the compound before it ever reaches the rest of the body—a process known as the first-pass effect. Sublingual delivery completely bypasses this loop, draining directly into the internal jugular and facial veins to provide an immediate effect.

Injections

Advantages of parenteral routes

  • Acts faster
  • Useful in emergencies
  • Works when the patient is unconscious, uncooperative, vomiting or has diarrhoea
  • No first-pass metabolism
  • Digestive juices can't destroy the drug
  • Bad-tasting drugs can be given
  • Drugs that aren't absorbed orally can be given
  • No food–drug interactions

Disadvantages of parenteral routes

  • The drug has to be sterile
  • Aseptic precautions are needed
  • Sterilising and injecting add to the cost
  • Invasive and painful
  • A trained person has to give it
  • Can irritate or injure tissue at the site
  • Hard to reverse once given, especially IV
Intramuscular
Aqueous or oily (depot) solutions. Faster than subcutaneous. Avoid in patients on anticoagulants: haematoma.
Intravenous
Immediate effect, 100% bioavailability, dose can be titrated. Aqueous solutions only (never suspensions). Riskiest route: thrombophlebitis, air embolism.

Inhalation and nasal

Inhalation
General anaesthetics, Salbutamol, Glucocorticoids, Ipratropium, Cromolyn sodium
Huge alveolar surface: very fast. Stop inhaling and the drug leaves in breath, so control is minute-to-minute.
Nasal spray
Desmopressin, GnRH agonists, Azelastine, Fluticasone, Cromolyn sodium
Skips gut and liver. KDT names desmopressin and GnRH agonists for systemic use.

Metered dose inhalers: advantages

  • Small and easy to carry
  • Acts quickly and is easy to use
  • Contents can't get contaminated
  • Dose can be controlled
  • Smaller dose, so milder systemic side effects

Metered dose inhalers: disadvantages

  • Fairly costly
  • Can irritate the airway locally
  • Pressing and breathing in must be timed together; poor timing means too little drug reaches the lungs
  • Much of the dose is wasted, settling on the mouth and throat lining
  • Contains a propellant

Topical

For a local effect on skin or mucous membranes: mouth, eye, ear, nasopharynx, oropharynx, anal canal, bladder, urethra, vagina.

Dosage forms
Mouthwash, Dental gel, Paste, Ointment, Cream, Lotion, Powder, Gel, Liniment, Shampoo, Suppository, Pessary
For your clinicMouthwashes, dental gels and pastes are the topical forms you'll prescribe every week.

Inside a skin patch (TTS)

A patch releases drug through the skin at a steady rate for a systemic effect.

Glyceryl trinitrate, Fentanyl, Nicotine, Estradiol, Hyoscine, Clonidine, Isosorbide dinitrate

Lasts 1–3 days. Smooth blood levels, little first-pass loss, better compliance. Rotate the site to limit skin irritation.

Transdermal: advantages

  • Patients stick to treatment more easily
  • Skips first-pass metabolism
  • Easy to use; the patient applies it
  • Effect stops when the patch is removed
  • Releases drug at a steady rate
  • Milder systemic side effects

Transdermal: disadvantages

  • Costly
  • Local skin reactions: itching, dermatitis, sensitisation
  • A carelessly thrown-away patch can harm a child who sticks it on

New drug delivery systems

Aim: get the drug to its target at the right rate and concentration, for the most benefit and the fewest adverse effects.

Why use them

  • Safer, fewer ADRs
  • Drug goes to the site of action (tumour cells)
  • Better bioavailability
  • Peptides and proteins arrive intact
  • Better compliance
  • Old drugs re-patented in new forms

Drawback

  • Very expensive
Examples
Liposomes, Prodrugs, Transdermal patches, Sustained release, Aerosols, Insulin pumps, PCA morphine, IUD, Ocusert, Monoclonal antibodies, RBCs as carriers, Microspheres, Gene therapy, PUVA
