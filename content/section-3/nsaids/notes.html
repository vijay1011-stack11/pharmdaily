Chapter 15 · NSAIDs and antipyretic-analgesics

Pain, fever and swelling

One enzyme, cyclooxygenase, explains almost everything here: why NSAIDs work, and why they hurt the stomach, kidney and platelets.

Definition

NSAIDs are drugs that relieve pain, lower fever and reduce inflammation by blocking cyclooxygenase (COX), which stops prostaglandin (PG) synthesis. Unlike morphine, they don't depress the CNS and don't cause physical dependence.
Pain → algesia
A drug that relieves it is an analgesic
Fever → pyrexia
A drug that lowers it is an antipyretic
Swelling → inflammation
A drug that reduces it is an anti-inflammatory
AnalgesicsOpioid (morphine) and non-opioid (NSAIDs).
Anti-inflammatory drugsSteroids and non-steroidal. Hence the name NSAID.

History: willow bark (Salix alba) → sodium salicylate (1875) → aspirin, acetylsalicylic acid (1899) → phenylbutazone (1949) → indomethacin (1963).

Mechanism of action

NSAIDs inhibit cyclooxygenase, so arachidonic acid is not turned into prostaglandins and thromboxane. They do not block lipoxygenase.

↓phospholipase A₂

Lipoxygenase pathwayLOXnot blocked↓LeukotrienesLTB₄, LTC₄, LTD₄

COX-1 · constitutive

  • Always present: stomach, kidney, platelets, lung, liver
  • Makes housekeeping PGs
  • Protects gastric mucosa
  • Keeps renal blood flow
  • TXA₂ for platelet function
  • Blocking it → side effects

COX-2 · inducible

  • Hardly present normally
  • Switched on at injury by cytokines (IL-1, TNF-α)
  • PGs of pain, inflammation, fever
  • Also constitutive in brain, kidney, uterus
  • Blocking it → benefit
Who blocks what
  • Non-selective: COX-1 + COX-2 → benefit plus gastric, platelet and kidney effects
  • Selective: COX-2 only → benefit with less gastric damage, but kidney COX-2 is still hit

Pharmacological actions

Nearly every action of an NSAID, good or bad, comes from less prostaglandin. Aspirin is the prototype, so its actions are described first; the rest of the group shares them to different degrees.

Benefits from PG block

  • Analgesia
  • Antipyresis
  • Anti-inflammatory action
  • Relief of dysmenorrhoea
  • Antiplatelet (antithrombotic) action
  • Closure of patent ductus arteriosus

Toxicities from the same block

  • Gastric mucosal damage
  • Bleeding: platelet function inhibited
  • Less renal blood flow: Na⁺ and water retention
  • Delayed or prolonged labour
  • Asthma and anaphylactoid reactions in susceptible people

1. Analgesic action

Peripherally, PGs sensitise nerve endings to bradykinin and histamine
+
Centrally, PGs lower the threshold of pain circuits
↓NSAIDs block both
Pain intensity falls: analgesia
Points to write in the exam
  • Aspirin is a weaker analgesic than morphine: 600 mg of aspirin roughly equals 60 mg of codeine
  • Works well on pain from inflammation and tissue injury: joints, muscles, connective tissue, skin, teeth
  • Works poorly on visceral and ischaemic pain
  • Mainly a peripheral action: dulls pain receptors and stops PG sensitisation of nerve endings
  • A smaller central (subcortical) action raises the pain threshold
  • No sedation, no tolerance, no dependence

2. Antipyretic action

Infection or injury → pyrogens: IL-1, TNF-α, interferons
↓
PGE₂ made in the hypothalamus
↓
Thermostat set higher: more heat made, heat loss shut down → fever
↓NSAIDs block PGE₂
Thermostat resets: sweating and skin vasodilation bring temperature down
Points to write in the exam
  • The hypothalamus balances heat production and heat loss
  • NSAIDs lower fever by increasing heat loss; they don't reduce heat production
  • Normal body temperature is not lowered
  • Useless in heat stroke: there the thermostat is not reset, so only external cooling works

3. Anti-inflammatory action

  • Blocks PG synthesis at the site of injury
  • Needs high doses of aspirin: 3–6 g/day, or about 100 mg/kg/day
  • Suppresses the signs of inflammation: pain, tenderness, swelling, vasodilation, leucocyte infiltration
  • Strength roughly follows how strongly each drug blocks COX
  • Other mechanisms: quenches free radicals; reduces adhesion molecules, GM-CSF and IL-6; stabilises leucocyte lysosomes; opposes kinins
  • Disease progression is not changed in RA, osteoarthritis or rheumatic fever: only symptoms improve

4. Gastrointestinal tract

Aspirin (pKa 3.5) is un-ionised in stomach acid → diffuses into mucosal cells
↓
Inside the cell (pH 7.1) it ionises and gets trapped ("ion trapping")
↓
Focal mucosal necrosis → erosive gastritis, acute ulcers, small bleeds
Why every NSAID can damage the stomach
  • COX-1 makes gastroprotective PGE₂ and PGI₂
  • Without them: less mucus and HCO₃⁻, more acid, less mucosal blood flow (ischaemia)
  • Local irritation adds back-diffusion of H⁺ into the mucosa
  • Result: aggressive factors rise, defensive factors fall
  • Symptoms: epigastric distress, nausea, vomiting; more occult blood in stools
  • High doses also stimulate the CTZ, causing vomiting

Less gastric damage

  • Paracetamol: very weak COX blocker, practically free of gastric toxicity
  • Selective COX-2 inhibitors
  • Misoprostol (a stable PG analogue) given alongside

Doesn't fully solve it

  • Soluble (buffered) aspirin: less local irritation, but ulcers and bleeding are not much lower

5. Blood and platelets (antiplatelet action)

Vessel wallProstacyclin (PGI₂)vasodilation, stops platelets clumping
PlateletThromboxane A₂vasoconstriction, makes platelets clumpaspirin blocks
  • NSAIDs block both TXA₂ and PGI₂, but the effect on platelet TXA₂ (COX-1) wins, so platelets clump less
  • Aspirin acetylates platelet COX irreversibly, and does this in the portal blood before the liver deacetylates it
  • Platelets have no nucleus, so they can't make new COX: bleeding time roughly doubles and stays long for about a week (platelet turnover)
  • Even small doses work; this is why aspirin is antithrombotic
  • Long-term large doses also cut liver synthesis of clotting factors → bleeding; prevented with vitamin K
  • Raises the risk of surgical bleeding and bleeding with anticoagulants

6. Kidney

In a healthy person the renal effects are small. When renal perfusion is low (CHF, hypovolaemia, cirrhosis, kidney disease, patients on diuretics), the kidney leans on its own PGs to keep blood flow and excrete Na⁺. NSAIDs remove that support in three ways:

1. COX-1 block → less renal blood flow and GFR → can worsen renal failure
+
2. COX-2 block (juxtaglomerular) → Na⁺ and water retained → edema
+
3. Habitual long-term use → papillary necrosis
Also
  • Blunt the effect of diuretics and antihypertensives
  • Analgesic nephropathy: after years of heavy analgesic use, especially combinations. Papillary necrosis, tubular atrophy, renal fibrosis. Phenacetin was blamed and banned.

7. Uterus

Dysmenorrhoea

  • PGs from the myometrium cause menstrual cramps
  • NSAIDs lower uterine PG: excellent relief in 60–70%, partial in the rest
  • Headache, muscle ache and nausea also ease; heavy flow may normalise

Labour

  • A sudden rise in uterine PG triggers labour and keeps it going
  • NSAIDs delay and slow labour
  • More postpartum bleeding

8. Ductus arteriosus

In the newborn

  • The ductus is a fetal shunt from pulmonary artery to aorta, kept open by local PGE₂ and PGI₂
  • It normally closes at birth
  • If it stays open, small doses of indomethacin or aspirin close it in most babies within hours

In late pregnancy

  • NSAIDs can close the ductus prematurely in the fetus
  • So avoid them near term

9. Respiration (aspirin)

Therapeutic anti-inflammatory doses: respiratory centre stimulated directly and by extra CO₂
↓
Hyperventilation washes out CO₂ → respiratory alkalosis, compensated by the kidney losing HCO₃⁻
↓adults on 3–5 g/day sit in compensated respiratory alkalosis
Toxic doses: respiration depressed while CO₂ keeps rising → respiratory acidosis
+
Lactic, pyruvic and acetoacetic acids plus salicylic acid pile up → uncompensated metabolic acidosis

Bronchi: PGE₂ and PGI₂ dilate, LTC₄ and LTD₄ constrict. Blocking COX may push arachidonic acid towards leukotrienes and trigger asthma in sensitive people; this is suspected, not proven.

10. Metabolic effects (high-dose aspirin)

  • Uncouples oxidative phosphorylation, mainly in skeletal muscle → more heat produced
  • More glucose used → blood sugar falls, especially in diabetics; liver glycogen is used up
  • Toxic doses stimulate the sympathetic centre → blood sugar rises
  • Negative nitrogen balance
  • Interferes with urate excretion and blocks the uricosuric effect of probenecid

11. Cardiovascular system

  • No direct action on heart or vessels
  • Doses that raise metabolic rate increase cardiac output indirectly
  • Toxic doses depress the vasomotor centre: BP may fall
  • Na⁺ and water retention can precipitate CHF when cardiac reserve is low

Uses of NSAIDs
1 · Analgesic

Mild to moderate pain

  • Headache, including mild migraine
  • Backache, myalgia, joint pain, pulled muscle
  • Toothache, neuralgia, dysmenorrhoea, post-operative pain
  • Aspirin 0.3–0.6 g every 6–8 h; analgesia peaks near 1 g as a single dose
  • Pain with little inflammation: paracetamol or low-dose ibuprofen
  • Post-operative, dental, renal colic and fracture pain: injectable ketorolac or diclofenac
2 · Antipyretic

Fever of any cause

  • Fever of any cause
  • Same dose as for analgesia
  • Paracetamol is preferred because it is safer
  • Antipyretic of choice in children: no Reye’s syndrome
  • Not useful in heat stroke: only external cooling lowers the temperature
3 · Acute rheumatic fever

Aspirin is the first drug in every case

  • Aspirin is the first drug in every case
  • Dose: 3–5 g/day (75–100 mg/kg/day) in divided doses
  • Marked relief in 1–3 days
  • Reduce the dose after 4–7 days
  • Maintenance 50 mg/kg/day for 2–3 weeks, or until signs of active disease (raised ESR) settle
  • Withdraw slowly over 2 weeks
  • Other drugs are added only if aspirin fails or the case is severe; corticosteroids act faster
  • Does not change nodules, carditis, valve damage, chorea or the course of the disease
4 · Arthritis

Rheumatoid arthritis and osteoarthritis: symptom relief only

  • Rheumatoid arthritis: aspirin 3–5 g/day relieves pain, swelling and morning stiffness
  • Disease progress is not changed
  • Large doses are poorly tolerated long-term, so newer NSAIDs are preferred now
  • Osteoarthritis: paracetamol is the first choice; aspirin is rarely used
  • Also used in ankylosing spondylitis, bursitis and acute gout (naproxen, piroxicam, indomethacin)
5 · MI and stroke prophylaxis

Now aspirin's most important use

  • Now aspirin’s most important use
  • Low-dose aspirin: 75–150 mg/day
  • Blocks platelet aggregation
  • Lowers the risk of myocardial infarction in coronary artery disease
  • Lowers the risk of stroke in transient ischaemic attacks and atrial fibrillation
  • Also used after unstable angina
6 · Dysmenorrhoea

Uterine PGs cause the cramps

  • Uterine PGs cause the menstrual cramps
  • NSAIDs lower uterine PG levels
  • Excellent relief in 60–70% of women, partial in the rest
  • Headache, muscle ache and nausea also improve
  • Mephenamic acid is especially effective
7 · Patent ductus arteriosus

Medical closure in the newborn

  • The fetal ductus is held open by PGE₂ and PGI₂
  • It should close after birth
  • If it stays open: small doses of indomethacin or aspirin
  • Closes in most babies within a few hours
8 · Prevention of preeclampsia

Correcting the TXA₂–PGI₂ balance

  • Excess TXA₂ over PGI₂ is believed to cause pregnancy-induced hypertension and preeclampsia
  • Low-dose aspirin is used to prevent it in high-risk women
For your clinicNSAIDs in dentistry: irreversible pulpitis, apical periodontitis, acute alveolar abscess, infected cyst, sinusitis, TMJ arthritis, MPDS, after tooth extraction, dry socket, recurrent aphthous ulcers, lichen planus. For post-extraction pain: injectable ketorolac or diclofenac, or an oral propionic acid drug. Piroxicam is also used in dentistry.

Paracetamol uses

  • The most used over-the-counter analgesic: headache, mild migraine, musculoskeletal pain, dysmenorrhoea
  • First-choice analgesic in osteoarthritis
  • Poor when inflammation dominates, as in RA
  • One of the best antipyretics, especially in children

ADRs of NSAIDs
1 · Gastric damage

The most important adverse effect of aspirin

  • The most important adverse effect of aspirin
  • At analgesic doses (0.5–1.5 g/day): epigastric distress, nausea, vomiting, more occult blood in stools
  • With continued use: erosive gastritis, peptic ulcer, bleeding, perforation
  • Cause: loss of COX-1 gastroprotective PGs, plus ion trapping and H⁺ back-diffusion
  • Every NSAID does this, to different extents
  • Much less with paracetamol and COX-2 inhibitors
2 · Bleeding

Platelets can't clump

  • Irreversible block of platelet TXA₂
  • Bleeding time doubles and stays long for about a week
  • Long-term high doses also lower clotting factors
  • Profuse gastric bleeding occurs rarely
  • Higher risk with anticoagulants and around surgery or extraction
3 · Kidney

Na⁺ and water retention

  • Dose-dependent Na⁺ and water retention → edema
  • Raises BP; can precipitate CHF
  • Worse in CHF, cirrhosis, hypovolaemia, renal disease and patients on diuretics
  • Interstitial nephritis, chronic renal failure
  • Papillary necrosis: rare; analgesic nephropathy after years of heavy use
4 · Hypersensitivity and asthma

Not common, but can be serious

  • Rashes, fixed drug eruption, urticaria, rhinorrhoea
  • Angioedema, asthma, anaphylactoid reactions
  • People who react to aspirin react to chemically unrelated NSAIDs too, so it is not true allergy
  • Suspected cause: arachidonic acid diverted to leukotrienes
  • Test dose is a must
5 · Pregnancy and labour

Avoid near term

  • Delayed and prolonged labour
  • More postpartum haemorrhage
  • Premature closure of the fetal ductus arteriosus
  • Repeated aspirin in pregnancy may cause low birth weight babies
  • Avoid near term
6 · Salicylism

At anti-inflammatory doses, 3–5 g/day

  • Seen at anti-inflammatory doses, 3–5 g/day
  • Dizziness, tinnitus, vertigo
  • Reversible loss of hearing and vision
  • Excitement, mental confusion
  • Hyperventilation, electrolyte imbalance
7 · Reye's syndrome and liver

Children with viral infections

  • Aspirin in children with chickenpox or influenza → Reye’s syndrome
  • Features: vomiting, liver damage, encephalopathy
  • High mortality, about 35%
  • So paediatric aspirin formulations are banned in India and the UK
  • Raised liver enzymes in children with RA on aspirin
  • Long-term high doses can injure the liver in adults too
8 · Acute salicylate poisoning

More common in children

  • More common in children
  • Fatal dose: 15–30 g in adults, much less in children
  • Features: vomiting, dehydration, electrolyte imbalance, acidotic breathing
  • Hyperpyrexia, high or low blood sugar, petechial haemorrhages
  • Restlessness, delirium, hallucinations, convulsions, coma
  • Death from respiratory failure and circulatory collapse
  • Treatment (no antidote): external cooling
  • IV fluids with Na⁺, K⁺, HCO₃⁻ and glucose, guided by repeated tests
  • Gastric lavage, dialysis
  • Vitamin K and blood transfusion if bleeding

Paracetamol overdose

Normal dose: conjugated with glucuronic acid and sulphate, excreted in urine. Safe and well tolerated; nausea and rashes occasionally, leukopenia rarely.
↓overdose: >150 mg/kg or >10 g in adults; fatal above 250 mg/kg
Conjugation saturates; more drug goes to the minor pathway; hepatic glutathione runs out
↓
Toxic metabolite NABQI binds covalently to liver (and kidney tubule) proteins
↓12–18 hours
Centrilobular hepatic necrosis, sometimes renal tubular necrosis and hypoglycaemia; jaundice from day 2
Treatment
  • Early: induce vomiting or gastric lavage; activated charcoal to stop absorption
  • Antidote: N-acetylcysteine, IV or oral. It refills glutathione and stops NABQI binding to liver cells.
  • The earlier the better; almost useless if started 16 hours or more after ingestion

Chronic alcoholics: even 5 g in a day can damage the liver, because alcohol induces CYP2E1, which makes NABQI. Not recommended in premature infants for fear of liver toxicity.

Paracetamol: why it's different

Action
  • De-ethylated active metabolite of phenacetin
  • Raises pain threshold centrally
  • Weak COX blocker in tissues, strong in the brain
  • Good, quick antipyretic; negligible anti-inflammatory action

Advantages over aspirin

  • Little gastric irritation; usable in ulcer patients
  • Doesn't prolong bleeding time
  • Hypersensitivity rare
  • No metabolic or acid–base disturbance
  • All ages, pregnancy, lactation
  • Few drug interactions

Selective COX-2 inhibitors

Advantages

  • Little GI ulceration
  • No bleeding tendency

Disadvantages

  • More heart attacks and strokes
  • Kidney COX-2 still blocked: Na⁺ and water retention, edema, high BP, can precipitate CHF

Drug-specific adverse effects

Indomethacin
GI and CNS effects in up to 50%: gastric irritation, nausea, anorexia, bleeding, diarrhoea. Frontal headache is very common; dizziness, ataxia, confusion, hallucinations, depression, psychosis. Also leukopenia and rashes.
Mephenamic acid
Diarrhoea is the main dose-related effect. Epigastric distress, but little gut bleeding. Rashes, dizziness. Haemolytic anaemia: rare but serious.
Ketorolac
Nausea, abdominal pain, dyspepsia, ulceration, loose stools, drowsiness, headache, pruritus, pain at injection site, raised liver enzymes, fluid retention. Not beyond 5 days.
Piroxicam
More GI effects than ibuprofen; rashes and pruritus in about 1%
Lornoxicam
Nausea, abdominal pain, visual disturbances, tinnitus, insomnia
Nefopam
Anticholinergic (dry mouth, urinary retention, blurred vision) and sympathomimetic (tachycardia, nervousness) effects; nausea limits the dose. Contraindicated in epilepsy.
