Chapter 29 · Sedative-hypnotics

Calming down and falling asleep

What sedatives and hypnotics are, how normal sleep is built, and the four groups of drugs used for insomnia.

Two definitions

Sedative: a drug that reduces excitement and anxiety without inducing sleep. It decreases responsiveness and motor activity. Some drowsiness may occur.
Hypnotic: a drug that induces and/or maintains sleep similar to normal arousable sleep.

"Hypnotic" here has nothing to do with hypnosis, the trance-like state where a person is under another's control.

Where the drugs differ
  • Quick onset, short action, steep dose-response curve: better as hypnotics
  • Slow onset, flatter curve: better as sedatives
  • Barbiturates walk the whole ladder. Benzodiazepines have a low ceiling: they don't reach anaesthesia or coma when given orally
  • Most important use of this class: treatment of insomnia

Normal sleep

Sleep runs in cycles of about 80–100 minutes: stages 0 → 4, then REM, repeated through the night.

Stage 0 · awake1–2%
Lying down until sleep, plus short night awakenings. EEG: α waves (eyes closed), β waves (eyes open).
Stage 1 · dozing3–6%
α waves mixed with θ waves. Eye movements fewer; neck muscles relax.
Stage 2 · unequivocal sleep40–50%
θ waves with sleep spindles; K complexes on sensory stimulation. Easily aroused.
Stage 3 · deep sleep transition5–8%
θ and δ waves, spindles. Not easily aroused.
Stage 4 · cerebral sleep10–20%
δ waves dominate. Hard to arouse. Night terrors happen here.
REM · paradoxical sleep20–30%
Darting eye movements, dreams and nightmares. Heart rate, BP and breathing irregular; muscles fully relaxed.
Slow-wave sleep (SWS) = stages 3 + 4 together. Stages 1–4 are non-REM (NREM) sleep.
